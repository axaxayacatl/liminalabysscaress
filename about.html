CYBORGISM / A BRIEF HISTORY

Cyborgism emerges from the historical intersection of cybernetics, biology, engineering, and technological intervention in the human organism.

Its foundations reach into the mid-twentieth century, when cybernetics began examining communication, feedback, and control across living organisms and mechanical systems. Norbert Wiener, Warren McCulloch, and Walter Pitts helped establish the scientific language through which biological and computational processes could be understood in relation to one another.

In 1960, Manfred E. Clynes and Nathan S. Kline introduced the term cyborg to describe an organism technologically adapted to operate in environments beyond its natural physiological limits. This engineering concept developed alongside advances in prosthetics, artificial organs, biomedical instrumentation, neural research, and human–machine interfaces.

Cyborgism subsequently expanded into artistic, philosophical, political, and cultural inquiry. Cybernetic art explored feedback and interaction as creative systems. Science fiction imagined altered bodies and technological identities. Feminist technoscience and posthuman theory questioned the boundaries separating human, machine, nature, and culture.

Its historical references include cybernetics, aerospace research, medical technology, cybernetic sculpture, interactive systems, posthumanism, and the theoretical work of Donna Haraway, N. Katherine Hayles, Anne Balsamo, and Chris Hables Gray.

Cyborgism is not a single aesthetic or doctrine. It is an evolving historical field concerned with the technological transformation of organisms, the integration of artificial systems into biological life, and the changing definition of the human.
